Modelling Motor Protein Motion

Stochastic Dynamics and Directed Transport

K. Myhre · S. Blom · L. Bekkely

Motor proteins play an important role in transporting molecular cargo within cells. Their ability to produce directed motion from thermal fluctuations makes them an interesting example of stochastic processes in biological systems. In this project, we investigate how directed motion can emerge from random walks in asymmetric potential landscapes. By modelling the movement of particles under different potentials and temperatures, we study how thermal fluctuations and potential asymmetry influence particle dynamics.

Main question

How can stochastic thermal motion produce directed transport when interacting with an asymmetric potential landscape?

01

Diffusion and Random Walks

We begin by considering the diffusion equation. For an initial distribution $h(x)$, the solution can be written using the Gaussian heat kernel:

$$ \phi(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} h(y) \exp\left( -\frac{(x-y)^2}{4Dt} \right) \,dy $$

This solution satisfies

$$ \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t} = D \frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial x^2}. $$

As $t \rightarrow 0$, the Gaussian kernel approaches a Dirac delta distribution:

$$ \phi(x,t) \rightarrow h(x). $$

This provides the theoretical foundation for describing particle motion as a stochastic diffusion process. The stochastic dynamics are implemented as a discrete random walk. At each time step, a particle can move left, remain at its current position, or move right, with transition probabilities determined by the potential energy at neighbouring positions.

$$ p^+ = \frac{e^{-\beta V(x+1)}} {e^{-\beta V(x-1)} +e^{-\beta V(x)} +e^{-\beta V(x+1)}} $$
$$ p^- = \frac{e^{-\beta V(x-1)}} {e^{-\beta V(x-1)} +e^{-\beta V(x)} +e^{-\beta V(x+1)}} $$
$$ p^0 = 1-p^+-p^-. $$

For a constant potential, the three probabilities are equal and the resulting motion corresponds to an unbiased random walk. Introducing a non-uniform potential changes these transition probabilities and allows the stochastic dynamics to be influenced by the energy landscape.

02

Potential Landscapes

The particle dynamics are influenced by the potential energy landscape $V(x)$. At thermal equilibrium, the probability of finding a particle at a particular position follows the Boltzmann distribution:

$$ P(x) = \frac{e^{-\beta V(x)}}{Z}, \qquad \beta = \frac{1}{k_B T}. $$

Here, $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant and $T$ is the temperature. The parameter $\beta$ describes the relative importance of the potential energy compared with thermal fluctuations. At high temperature, thermal fluctuations dominate the dynamics, while at low temperature the potential has a stronger influence on particle motion.

High temperature

Thermal fluctuations dominate the dynamics and particles behave more like unbiased random walkers.

Low temperature

The potential becomes more important and particles are more strongly influenced by potential minima and barriers.

Several different landscapes were considered to investigate how the potential influences particle motion. A constant potential produces approximately unbiased diffusion, while a linear potential introduces a preferred direction. Periodic potentials create repeating wells and barriers, and a quartic potential strongly confines particles around the centre.

03

Directed Transport

A central part of the project is the flashing ratchet model. The system alternates between a flat potential, where particles can diffuse freely, and an asymmetric periodic potential. During the flat-potential phase, thermal fluctuations allow particles to spread out. When the asymmetric potential is switched on, particles tend to move towards nearby potential minima.

Repeating this process can result in a net displacement even though the underlying particle motion is stochastic. The mechanism therefore combines thermal fluctuations with spatial asymmetry and time-dependent switching.

The direction of transport depends strongly on the asymmetry of the potential. The average particle current is measured using the difference between rightward and leftward transitions:

$$ J = \frac{n^+ - n^-}{N_p}. $$
α < 0.5

Positive current and preferential motion to the right.

α = 0.5

Symmetric potential and approximately zero net current.

α > 0.5

Negative current and preferential motion to the left.

The duration of each flashing phase also influences the resulting current. If the potential is switched too quickly, particles do not have enough time to respond to the potential landscape. For intermediate flashing intervals, a stronger directed current can emerge, while very large flashing intervals can reduce the current again.

$$ J_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{N_x}{4T_p} \left[ \operatorname{erfc} \left( \frac{\alpha N_x}{2} \sqrt{\frac{3}{T_p}} \right) - \operatorname{erfc} \left( \frac{(1-\alpha)N_x}{2} \sqrt{\frac{3}{T_p}} \right) \right]. $$

This analytical expression provides a theoretical prediction for the average current and can be compared with the numerical simulations.

04

Numerical Results

The numerical simulations were compared with the analytical expression for the average current. For a range of asymmetry parameters, the numerical and analytical results show good agreement. Small deviations can occur because the numerical model uses a discrete lattice and a finite number of particles, while stochastic fluctuations introduce variation between simulations.

Increasing $\beta$ strengthens the influence of the potential relative to thermal fluctuations. At very small $\beta$, the potential is weak compared with thermal fluctuations and the numerical current approaches zero. For larger $\beta$, the numerical results become increasingly similar to the analytical prediction.

Key observation

Directed transport does not require deterministic particle motion. Stochastic thermal motion combined with spatial asymmetry and time-dependent switching can produce a measurable net current.

The model is deliberately simplified and does not reproduce the full molecular complexity of biological motor proteins. Real molecular motors involve chemical reactions, conformational changes, multiple interacting states, and complex cellular environments. Nevertheless, the model demonstrates how directed motion can emerge from stochastic dynamics when spatial asymmetry and time-dependent driving break the symmetry of otherwise random motion.

Overall, the simulations illustrate how temperature, potential shape, asymmetry, and flashing time influence both particle distributions and net current. The comparison between numerical and analytical results also shows how simplified theoretical models can describe the average behaviour of a stochastic system while still having limitations at the level of individual trajectories.
