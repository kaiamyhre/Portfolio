Brownian motion describes the seemingly random movement of microscopic particles caused by thermal fluctuations in their surroundings. While this motion has no preferred direction on its own, biological systems such as molecular motors are able to convert these fluctuations into directed transport.

In this project, we investigate how an asymmetric and time-dependent potential can produce a net particle current from otherwise random motion. We first establish the connection between diffusion, thermal energy and particle motion before developing a numerical model of a Brownian ratchet.

Finally, the numerical results are compared with an analytical solution to determine when the simplified theoretical model provides an accurate description of the system.

Main question

How can stochastic thermal motion produce directed transport when interacting with an asymmetric, time-dependent potential landscape?

01

From Diffusion to Particle Motion

At microscopic scales, particles are constantly affected by thermal fluctuations from their surroundings. This produces Brownian motion: an irregular and seemingly random movement that can be described statistically rather than by predicting the trajectory of each individual particle.

We begin with the one-dimensional diffusion equation,

$$ \frac{\partial \phi(x,t)}{\partial t} = D\frac{\partial^2\phi(x,t)}{\partial x^2}, $$

where $\phi(x,t)$ describes the particle distribution and $D$ is the diffusion coefficient. For an initial particle distribution $h(x)$, the solution can be written using the Gaussian heat kernel,

$$ \phi(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} h(y) \exp\left( -\frac{(x-y)^2}{4Dt} \right) \,dy. $$

In the project, we verified that this expression satisfies the diffusion equation by differentiating it with respect to time and position. In the limit $t\rightarrow0$, the Gaussian kernel approaches a Dirac delta distribution,

$$ \lim_{t\to0} \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}} e^{-\frac{(x-y)^2}{4Dt}} = \delta(x-y), $$

which gives $\phi(x,0)=h(x)$. The function $h(x)$ can therefore be interpreted as the initial particle distribution. As time increases, the Gaussian kernel broadens, describing how an initially localized population spreads through random thermal motion.

From continuous diffusion to a random walk

To simulate this process numerically, we represent the particles as random walkers on a one-dimensional lattice. At each time step, a particle can move one position to the left, remain at its current position, or move one position to the right.

In a flat energy landscape these movements are equally likely, but introducing a potential $V(x)$ changes the probability of each transition. The relative probability of occupying a state is determined by the Boltzmann distribution,

$$ P(x) = \frac{e^{-\beta V(x)}}{Z}, \qquad \beta=\frac{1}{k_BT}, $$

where $Z$ is the partition function, $k_B$ is the Boltzmann constant and $T$ is the temperature. Applying this distribution to the three possible positions gives the transition probabilities

$$ \begin{aligned} p^+ &= \frac{e^{-\beta V(x+1)}} {e^{-\beta V(x-1)} +e^{-\beta V(x)} +e^{-\beta V(x+1)}}, \\[8pt] p^0 &= \frac{e^{-\beta V(x)}} {e^{-\beta V(x-1)} +e^{-\beta V(x)} +e^{-\beta V(x+1)}}, \\[8pt] p^- &= \frac{e^{-\beta V(x-1)}} {e^{-\beta V(x-1)} +e^{-\beta V(x)} +e^{-\beta V(x+1)}}. \end{aligned} $$

The parameter $\beta$ determines the competition between thermal fluctuations and the potential landscape. When the thermal energy is much larger than the local potential differences,

$$ k_BT \gg \Delta V, $$

$\beta$ is small and $e^{-\beta\Delta V}\approx1$. The three transitions therefore become approximately equally probable,

$$ p^+ \approx p^0 \approx p^- \approx \frac{1}{3}. $$

The particles then behave approximately as unbiased random walkers. At low temperatures, the opposite occurs: potential-energy differences dominate over thermal fluctuations, making transitions toward energetically unfavorable positions increasingly unlikely. Particles therefore tend to remain near regions of low potential energy.

Numerical simulation in different potential landscapes

With these transition probabilities established, we implemented the stochastic model numerically in Python using NumPy, SciPy and Matplotlib. At each time step, the local potential determines the transition probabilities, while a random number determines the actual movement of each particle.

We initially simulated 10,000 particles in several different potential landscapes. A constant potential produced approximately symmetric diffusion, while a linear potential biased the movement in one direction. More complex potentials demonstrated how barriers and local minima can restrict the motion and confine particles to particular regions.

These simulations establish the two ingredients needed for the main part of the project: random motion generated by thermal fluctuations, and a potential landscape capable of biasing that motion. The next step is to combine them in a time-dependent asymmetric system and investigate whether random motion can produce a persistent net current.

02

Generating Directed Motion with a Brownian Ratchet

The central part of the project investigates a periodically switching potential. The particles alternate between two states: a constant potential $V_2$, where they undergo approximately free diffusion, and an asymmetric periodic potential $V_1$.

The asymmetry of $V_1$ is controlled by the parameter $\alpha$. This determines the relative distances between a potential maximum and the neighboring minima and therefore breaks the spatial symmetry of the system.

While $V_2$ is active, there is no preferred direction and the particles diffuse randomly. When the asymmetric potential $V_1$ is restored, the particles are driven toward nearby potential minima.

Because the potential is asymmetric, the distances to the neighboring minima are unequal. After the particles have spread through diffusion, they therefore do not have equal probabilities of reaching the minima on either side. Repeated switching can consequently convert random Brownian motion into a net directional particle current.

4

The trajectories illustrate the mechanism directly. During the free-diffusion stage, the particles make small random movements in both directions. When the asymmetric potential is activated, they move toward nearby minima. Repeating these two stages produces the characteristic step-like directed motion seen in the simulation.

03

Controlling the Particle Current

To quantify the directed transport, we define the normalized particle current from the difference between rightward and leftward transitions,

$$ J(t_i) = \frac{n^+(t_i)-n^-(t_i)}{N_p}, $$

where $n^+$ and $n^-$ are the number of particles moving to the right and left, respectively, and $N_p$ is the total number of particles. A positive value of $J$ therefore represents net transport to the right, while a negative value represents net transport to the left.

Potential asymmetry

The direction of the current is controlled by the asymmetry parameter $\alpha$. Changing $\alpha$ changes the geometry of the periodic potential and therefore which neighboring minimum is more easily reached after the particles diffuse.

$\alpha < 0.5$

Positive current and preferential motion to the right.

$\alpha = 0.5$

Symmetric potential and approximately zero net current.

$\alpha > 0.5$

Negative current and preferential motion to the left.

The simulations therefore show that reversing the spatial asymmetry also reverses the direction of transport. At $\alpha\approx0.5$, neither direction is preferred and the average current approaches zero.

Dependence on the switching period

Directed transport also depends strongly on the switching period $T_p$, which controls how long the particles are allowed to evolve before the potential changes.

For very short switching periods, particles have little time to respond to the potential or diffuse away from their initial positions, and the resulting current remains close to zero. Increasing $T_p$ allows the particles to spread farther before the asymmetric potential is restored, producing a stronger directional effect.

For sufficiently long switching periods, the behavior changes again as the particles have more time to redistribute before the potential switches. The simulations therefore demonstrate that directed transport is determined not only by spatial asymmetry, but also by the timescale at which the system is driven.

04

Numerical and Analytical Comparison

The numerical simulations allow us to investigate the particle current directly from a finite population of stochastic particles. To evaluate the model, we compare these results with an analytical expression for the average current,

$$ J_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{N_x}{4T_p} \left[ \operatorname{erfc} \left( \frac{\alpha N_x}{2} \sqrt{\frac{3}{T_p}} \right) - \operatorname{erfc} \left( \frac{(1-\alpha)N_x}{2} \sqrt{\frac{3}{T_p}} \right) \right]. $$

For the low-temperature regime investigated in the project, the numerical and analytical solutions show very good agreement when varying the asymmetry parameter $\alpha$. Both capture the change in direction as the potential passes through the symmetric case at $\alpha=0.5$.

Small differences between the two approaches are expected. The numerical model represents a finite number of particles on a discrete lattice and therefore contains both stochastic fluctuations and discretization effects. The analytical model, in contrast, describes an idealized continuous particle distribution.

The role of temperature

Temperature reveals an important limitation of the analytical approximation. The analytical expression does not explicitly contain the inverse-temperature parameter $\beta$, while the numerical simulations show that the particle current depends strongly on thermal energy.

For small $\beta$, corresponding to high thermal energy, the transition probabilities become increasingly similar and the particles behave almost as unbiased random walkers. The net numerical current therefore approaches zero for all values of $\alpha$.

As $\beta$ increases, the potential has a stronger influence on the particle motion and the numerical results become increasingly similar to the analytical prediction. The comparison therefore shows both where the analytical model performs well and where its assumptions become insufficient to capture the full stochastic dynamics.

Key observation

The analytical approximation agrees well with the numerical model when the potential strongly influences the particle dynamics, while the stochastic simulation also captures the temperature dependence that is absent from the analytical expression.

05

Conclusion

The simulations demonstrate how directed transport can emerge from fundamentally random particle motion. Brownian motion itself has no preferred direction, but combining thermal fluctuations with a periodically switching asymmetric potential breaks this symmetry and produces a measurable particle current.

The direction and magnitude of this current depend on the shape of the potential, the thermal energy and the switching timescale. Changing the asymmetry reverses the direction of transport, while changing the switching period determines how effectively random diffusion can be converted into net motion.

Comparison with the analytical model further demonstrates both the strengths and limitations of simplified theoretical descriptions. The numerical stochastic model reproduces the analytical behavior in suitable parameter regimes while also capturing finite-particle fluctuations, discretization effects and temperature dependence.

Through the project, we combined statistical physics, mathematical modelling and numerical simulation to connect microscopic random motion with macroscopic directed transport and investigate a simplified physical model for motor protein motion.
