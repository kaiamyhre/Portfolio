Thermal fluctuations cause microscopic particles to move randomly, but this random motion can be influenced by the surrounding energy landscape. In this project, we investigate how temperature and potential energy affect stochastic particle motion.

We begin with the diffusion equation and a discrete random-walk model. We then introduce Boltzmann-weighted transition probabilities and study several potential landscapes. Finally, we use an asymmetric, periodically switched potential to investigate how random motion can produce directed transport.

Main question

How can random thermal motion produce directed transport in an asymmetric potential?

01

Diffusion and Thermally Activated Motion

We first consider the one-dimensional diffusion equation

$$ \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t} = D\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial x^2}. $$

For an initial distribution $h(x)$, a solution is given by the Gaussian heat kernel

$$ \phi(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} h(y) e^{-(x-y)^2/(4Dt)} \,dy. $$

As $t\rightarrow0$, the Gaussian kernel approaches $\delta(x-y)$, giving $\phi(x,0)=h(x)$. This verifies that the solution satisfies the required initial condition.

We then describe particle motion as a discrete random walk. A particle can move one step left, remain at its current position, or move one step right. The probabilities are determined by the Boltzmann weights of the three possible states.

$$ p^+ = \frac{e^{-\beta V(x+1)}} { e^{-\beta V(x-1)} +e^{-\beta V(x)} +e^{-\beta V(x+1)} } $$
$$ p^- = \frac{e^{-\beta V(x-1)}} { e^{-\beta V(x-1)} +e^{-\beta V(x)} +e^{-\beta V(x+1)} } $$
$$ p^0=1-p^+-p^-. $$

The inverse temperature $\beta=1/(k_BT)$ controls how strongly the potential affects the motion. At high temperature, the three states become more equally probable. At low temperature, particles preferentially move towards lower-energy states.

High temperature

Thermal fluctuations dominate and the motion approaches an unbiased random walk.

Low temperature

Potential-energy differences strongly influence the particle motion.

We investigate four different potentials to see how the energy landscape changes the resulting particle distributions.

01

Constant

$$ V_1(x)=k $$

Gives approximately unbiased diffusion.

02

Linear

$$ V_2(x)=-kx $$

Favors motion towards lower potential.

03

Periodic

$$ V_3(x)=k \left( \frac{x}{15}-\cos\frac{x}{3} \right) $$

Creates repeating minima and barriers.

04

Quartic

$$ V_4(x)=kx^4 $$

Confines particles near $x=0$.

02

The Flashing Ratchet

We now introduce a periodically switched asymmetric potential. The system alternates between a constant potential and an asymmetric sawtooth potential.

During the constant-potential phase, particles diffuse through thermal fluctuations. When the asymmetric potential is switched on, particles tend to move towards nearby potential minima. Repeating this cycle can produce a net displacement.

1

Diffuse

Particles move through thermal fluctuations.

→
2

Switch

The asymmetric potential is activated.

→
3

Relax

Particles move towards potential minima.

→
4

Repeat

The cycle produces directed transport.

The net transport is quantified by the current

$$ J(t)= \frac{n^+(t)-n^-(t)}{N_p}, $$

where $n^+$ and $n^-$ are the numbers of particles moving right and left, respectively. The current is then averaged over one complete switching cycle.

03

Directed Transport

The asymmetry of the potential is controlled by the parameter $\alpha$. Changing $\alpha$ changes the shape of the sawtooth potential and can reverse the direction of transport.

In our simulations, the current is positive for $\alpha<0.5$ and negative for $\alpha>0.5$. At $\alpha=0.5$, the potential is symmetric and the net current approaches zero.

The switching time $T_p$ also affects the transport. For very short switching times, particles have little time to respond to the potential. At intermediate times, the current becomes stronger, while for sufficiently long switching times the current decreases again in our simulations.

04

Numerical and Analytical Results

To validate the numerical model, we compare the simulated current with an analytical expression:

$$ J_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{N_x}{4T_p} \left[ \operatorname{erfc} \left( \frac{\alpha N_x}{2} \sqrt{\frac{3}{T_p}} \right) - \operatorname{erfc} \left( \frac{(1-\alpha)N_x}{2} \sqrt{\frac{3}{T_p}} \right) \right]. $$

For the low-temperature regime investigated, the numerical and analytical results show good agreement as a function of $\alpha$. Small deviations arise from the finite number of particles, stochastic fluctuations and the discrete numerical model.

We finally investigate the role of temperature through $\beta$. At small $\beta$, thermal fluctuations dominate and the numerical current is strongly reduced. As $\beta$ increases, the potential has a stronger influence on the particle dynamics, and the numerical result approaches the analytical prediction more closely.

Key observation

Directed transport emerges from the combination of thermal fluctuations, spatial asymmetry and periodic switching.

05

Conclusion

We investigated how thermal fluctuations and potential landscapes determine stochastic particle motion. Starting from the diffusion equation, we developed a discrete random-walk model with Boltzmann-weighted transition probabilities.

The simulations showed how different potentials produce different particle distributions, and how temperature controls the balance between random motion and potential-driven motion.

By periodically switching an asymmetric potential on and off, random motion was converted into a measurable net current. The direction of this current depended on the asymmetry parameter, while its magnitude also depended on the switching time and temperature.

Comparison with the analytical expression showed good agreement within the investigated regime, while deviations demonstrated the effects of stochastic fluctuations and the discrete numerical model.
