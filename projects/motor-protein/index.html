Thermally Activated Particle Motion and Directed Transport

Diffusion, Boltzmann-weighted random walks, asymmetric potentials, and the emergence of directed particle motion

1. Introduction

The motion of microscopic particles is often dominated by thermal fluctuations. At finite temperature, particles constantly move due to random interactions with their surroundings. Even when there is no externally imposed direction, this random motion can lead to interesting transport phenomena when the particles interact with a non-uniform potential.

In this project, we investigate how thermal fluctuations influence particle motion and how an asymmetric potential can transform random motion into directed transport. We begin with the diffusion equation and a simple random walk, before introducing temperature-dependent transition probabilities based on the Boltzmann distribution.

Finally, we study a periodically switched asymmetric potential. This system acts as a type of flashing ratchet: particles move randomly when the potential is switched off, while the asymmetric potential biases their motion when it is switched on. We investigate how the resulting current depends on the asymmetry and the switching time, and compare our numerical results with an analytical expression.

Main question: How can random thermal motion produce a net directed current when the system contains an asymmetric and periodically switched potential?

2. The Diffusion Equation

We first consider the one-dimensional diffusion equation

$$\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t} = D\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial x^2},$$

where $\phi(x,t)$ describes the particle distribution and $D$ is the diffusion coefficient. The equation describes how an initially localized distribution spreads out over time.

A solution to this equation can be written as a convolution with a Gaussian kernel:

$$\phi(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} h(y) e^{-(x-y)^2/(4Dt)} \,dy.$$

Here, $h(y)$ is the initial particle distribution. We verified the solution by differentiating it with respect to both $t$ and $x$, showing that the two sides of the diffusion equation are equal.

The initial condition follows from the limiting behaviour of the Gaussian kernel:

$$\lim_{t\rightarrow0} \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}} e^{-(x-y)^2/(4Dt)} = \delta(x-y).$$

This gives $\phi(x,0)=h(x)$, meaning that the solution correctly reproduces the initial distribution.

3. Thermally Activated Random Walk

We next replace the continuous diffusion description with a discrete random walk. A particle can move one step to the left, one step to the right, or remain at its current position.

The probability of finding a particle at a position with potential energy $V(x)$ is related to the Boltzmann factor

$$P(x)\propto e^{-\beta V(x)},$$

where

$$\beta=\frac{1}{k_BT}.$$

The parameter $\beta$ therefore determines how strongly the potential influences the particle motion. A large $\beta$ corresponds to low temperature, while a small $\beta$ corresponds to high temperature.

For a particle at $x_0$, we assign Boltzmann weights to the three possible positions $x_0-h$, $x_0$, and $x_0+h$. The probability of moving right is

$$p_+ = \frac{e^{-\beta V(x_0+h)}} {e^{-\beta V(x_0-h)} +e^{-\beta V(x_0)} +e^{-\beta V(x_0+h)}}.$$

Similarly, the probabilities of staying and moving left are obtained from the corresponding Boltzmann weights.

Temperature limits

At high temperature, the potential differences become small compared with the thermal energy. The three possible states therefore have approximately equal probability:

$$p_+\approx p_0\approx p_-\approx\frac13.$$

The motion is then close to an unbiased random walk.

At low temperature, differences in potential energy become much more important. The particle strongly favours moves towards lower-energy states, while moves towards higher-energy states become unlikely.

4. Numerical Model

We simulate many particles on a one-dimensional lattice. At every time step, each particle is allowed to move one lattice spacing to the left, one spacing to the right, or remain at its current position.

The transition probabilities are recalculated from the local potential and the value of $\beta$. The simulations therefore combine deterministic information from the potential with stochastic thermal motion.

For the first simulations, we use

  • $h=1$ as the lattice spacing,
  • $\Delta t=1$ as the time step,
  • $10\,000$ particles,
  • $200$ time steps.

The particles are initially placed at $x=0$ and are then allowed to evolve according to the transition probabilities.

5. The Effect of the Potential Landscape

To investigate how the potential affects particle motion, we consider four different potential landscapes.

5.1 Constant potential

$$V_1(x)=k.$$

Since the potential is the same everywhere, it does not favour any particular direction. The transition probabilities are therefore independent of $\beta$. The resulting particle distribution is centred around the starting point and approaches a Gaussian shape, as expected for a random walk with many steps.

5.2 Linear potential

$$V_2(x)=-kx.$$

In this potential, the energy decreases as $x$ increases. The Boltzmann weights therefore favour motion towards the right.

At high temperature the thermal fluctuations dominate and the distribution remains relatively broad. As the temperature decreases, the potential becomes increasingly important and the particles become strongly biased towards lower potential energy.

5.3 Periodic potential

$$V_3(x)=k\left(\frac{x}{15}-\cos\left(\frac{x}{3}\right)\right).$$

This potential contains several local minima and barriers. Particles therefore tend to accumulate in the valleys of the potential, while thermal fluctuations determine how easily they can move between neighbouring minima.

At high temperature, particles have enough thermal energy to cross several potential barriers. At lower temperature, they become increasingly localized around the lowest accessible energy states.

5.4 Quartic potential

$$V_4(x)=kx^4.$$

The quartic potential has a minimum at $x=0$ and increases rapidly away from the origin. It therefore confines the particles near the centre.

Increasing $\beta$ makes the confinement more pronounced because thermal fluctuations become less important compared with the potential energy.

6. From Random Motion to Directed Transport

The previous simulations show that a potential can bias random particle motion. We now ask whether this bias can be used to create a net current.

To investigate this, we introduce a periodically switched asymmetric potential. The system alternates between two states:

  • Flat state: the potential is constant, so the particles undergo approximately unbiased thermal motion.
  • Asymmetric state: a sawtooth potential is switched on, causing particles to redistribute towards its minima.

The switching is repeated periodically. Because the potential is asymmetric, the relaxation of the particles is not equally likely to produce motion in both directions.

This mechanism is an example of a flashing ratchet: random thermal motion is combined with a periodically switched asymmetric potential to produce directed transport.

7. The Asymmetric Sawtooth Potential

The ratchet potential consists of an asymmetric sawtooth shape. The parameter $\alpha$ controls the asymmetry of the potential.

In the simulations, we use a system size of $N_x=100$ and compare different values of $\alpha$. Changing $\alpha$ changes the relative widths of the two sides of the potential wells and therefore changes the preferred direction of transport.

The potential is switched between the asymmetric state and a constant state with a characteristic switching time $T_p$. During the flat part of the cycle, particles can diffuse freely. When the asymmetric potential is restored, the particles preferentially move towards nearby minima.

8. Particle Trajectories

The effect of the switching mechanism can be seen directly by following individual particles.

While the potential is flat, the particles perform random motion. When the asymmetric potential is switched on, their motion becomes biased by the nearby potential minima. Repeating this process produces a systematic displacement over many cycles.

9. Measuring the Current

To quantify the directed transport, we define the current from the difference between the number of right-moving and left-moving particles:

$$J(t)=\frac{n^+(t)-n^-(t)}{N_p},$$

where $n^+$ is the number of particles moving to the right, $n^-$ is the number moving to the left, and $N_p$ is the total number of particles.

We calculate the average current over one complete switching cycle. A positive value corresponds to net motion in the positive $x$-direction, while a negative value corresponds to net motion in the opposite direction.

10. Asymmetry Controls the Direction

One of the most important parameters in the ratchet model is the asymmetry $\alpha$. We therefore investigate the current as a function of $\alpha$.

The numerical results show a change in the direction of transport around $\alpha=0.5$. For $\alpha<0.5$, the current has one sign, while for $\alpha>0.5$, the current changes sign. Around $\alpha=0.5$, the potential becomes symmetric and the net current approaches zero.

This demonstrates that spatial asymmetry is essential for the directed transport observed in the model.

11. Effect of the Switching Time

The switching time $T_p$ determines how long the system remains in each state before the potential is changed again.

For very short switching times, the particles do not have enough time to respond significantly to the potential before it is switched again. The resulting current is therefore small.

For intermediate switching times, the particles have enough time to respond to the asymmetric potential, producing a stronger directed current.

For sufficiently long switching times, the particles have more time to redistribute before the next switch. In our simulations, the current decreases again in this regime. This gives a non-monotonic dependence of the current on the switching time.

12. Numerical and Analytical Current

To test the numerical model, we compare the simulated current with an analytical expression:

$$ J_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{N_x}{4T_p} \left[ \operatorname{erfc} \left( \frac{\alpha N_x}{2} \sqrt{\frac{3}{T_p}} \right) - \operatorname{erfc} \left( \frac{(1-\alpha)N_x}{2} \sqrt{\frac{3}{T_p}} \right) \right]. $$

Here, $\operatorname{erfc}$ is the complementary error function. The analytical expression provides a theoretical prediction for the average current based on the parameters of the ratchet system.

12.1 Current as a function of asymmetry

For $T_p=500$ and $N_x=100$, the numerical and analytical results show very similar behaviour as $\alpha$ is varied. The current changes sign around the symmetric case $\alpha=0.5$.

The small deviations between the two curves can be attributed to the stochastic nature of the simulation, the finite number of particles, and the discrete lattice and time steps.

12.2 Current as a function of switching time

We also compare the current as a function of $T_p$. The numerical results reproduce the general behaviour predicted by the analytical expression, but larger deviations appear in this comparison.

Since the simulation contains a finite number of particles, statistical fluctuations remain visible in the numerical current. In addition, the simulation uses discrete positions and time steps, whereas the analytical description is based on a more continuous mathematical treatment.

13. Temperature Dependence of the Analytical Model

The stochastic simulations contain an explicit temperature dependence through the parameter $\beta$. The analytical expression above, however, does not contain $\beta$ explicitly.

We therefore investigate how the agreement between the numerical and analytical results changes when $\beta$ is varied.

For small $\beta$, corresponding to high temperature, thermal fluctuations are strong compared with the potential energy differences. The numerical current is consequently strongly reduced.

As $\beta$ increases, the potential has a stronger influence on the particle motion, and the numerical results move closer to the analytical prediction. This indicates that the analytical expression captures the regime represented by the low-temperature simulations more accurately than the high-temperature regime.

14. Model Limitations

The numerical model provides a simplified description of thermally activated particle motion. Several assumptions are made:

  • The particles move on a one-dimensional discrete lattice.
  • Only nearest-neighbour moves are allowed.
  • The transition probabilities are determined locally from the potential.
  • The number of particles is finite, so statistical fluctuations are present.
  • The dynamics are simulated using discrete time steps.
  • The ratchet potential is switched according to a prescribed periodic cycle.

These simplifications make the system computationally manageable while still allowing us to study the main physical mechanisms behind thermally driven transport.

15. Conclusion

This project investigated how thermal fluctuations and potential landscapes determine the motion of particles. We first verified a solution to the diffusion equation and connected the continuous diffusion description to a discrete random-walk model.

By assigning Boltzmann-weighted transition probabilities to neighbouring lattice sites, we introduced temperature-dependent particle motion. At high temperature, the particles behave approximately as an unbiased random walk, while at low temperature the potential has a much stronger influence on their motion.

We then introduced an asymmetric, periodically switched potential. The combination of thermal fluctuations, spatial asymmetry, and periodic switching produced a non-zero average particle current. The direction of the current depended on the asymmetry parameter $\alpha$, and the current showed a non-monotonic dependence on the switching time $T_p$.

Finally, we compared the numerical results with an analytical expression for the average current. The agreement was particularly good for the dependence on $\alpha$, while larger deviations appeared for some switching times and temperatures. These deviations illustrate the effects of finite particle number, stochastic fluctuations, and the discrete nature of the numerical model.

Overall, the simulations demonstrate how random thermal motion can be rectified by an asymmetric and time-dependent potential, producing directed transport without directly imposing a constant external force.
