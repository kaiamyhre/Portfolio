Modelling Motor Protein Motion

Stochastic Dynamics and Directed Transport

K. Myhre · S. Blom · L. Bekkely

Motor proteins play an important role in transporting molecular cargo within cells. Their ability to produce directed motion from thermal fluctuations makes them an interesting example of stochastic processes in biological systems.

In this project, we investigate how directed motion can emerge from stochastic dynamics using a simplified mathematical model. Particles are represented as random walkers on a one-dimensional lattice, where their movement is influenced by the surrounding potential landscape and temperature.

By studying different potentials and a time-dependent asymmetric potential, we investigate how thermal fluctuations, spatial asymmetry and switching time influence particle motion and produce a net current.

Main question

How can stochastic thermal motion produce directed transport when interacting with an asymmetric potential landscape?

01

Stochastic Motion and Potential Landscapes

We begin with the diffusion equation, which describes how a probability distribution evolves through time:

$$ \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t} = D \frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial x^2}. $$

For an initial distribution $h(x)$, the solution can be written using the Gaussian heat kernel:

$$ \phi(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} h(y) \exp\left( -\frac{(x-y)^2}{4Dt} \right) \,dy. $$

As $t \rightarrow 0$, the Gaussian kernel approaches a Dirac delta distribution, giving $\phi(x,t)\rightarrow h(x)$. This provides the theoretical foundation for describing particle motion as a stochastic diffusion process.

In the numerical model, particles perform a discrete random walk on a one-dimensional lattice. At each time step, a particle can move one step to the left, remain at its current position, or move one step to the right. The transition probabilities are determined by the potential energy of the three possible positions.

$$ p^+ = \frac{e^{-\beta V(x+1)}} { e^{-\beta V(x-1)} + e^{-\beta V(x)} + e^{-\beta V(x+1)} } $$
$$ p^- = \frac{e^{-\beta V(x-1)}} { e^{-\beta V(x-1)} + e^{-\beta V(x)} + e^{-\beta V(x+1)} } $$
$$ p^0 = 1-p^+-p^-. $$

The parameter $$ \beta=\frac{1}{k_BT} $$ determines the relative importance of the potential compared with thermal fluctuations. Small $\beta$ corresponds to stronger thermal fluctuations, while large $\beta$ gives the potential a stronger influence on the particle dynamics.

Small $\beta$

Thermal fluctuations dominate, making the motion more similar to an unbiased random walk.

Large $\beta$

The potential has a stronger influence on the transition probabilities and particle motion.

We investigate several potential landscapes to understand how different energy profiles influence stochastic motion.

02

The Flashing Ratchet

The main part of the project is the flashing ratchet model. The system alternates between a flat potential and an asymmetric periodic potential.

When the potential is switched off, particles diffuse freely due to thermal fluctuations. When the asymmetric potential is switched on, particles tend to move towards nearby potential minima. Repeating this process can produce a net displacement even though the individual particle motion remains stochastic.

Repeated cycles can produce a net current.

The average current is determined from the difference between rightward and leftward transitions:

$$ J = \frac{n^+-n^-}{N_p}. $$

The direction of transport depends on the asymmetry parameter $\alpha$. For the parameters investigated in our simulations, $\alpha<0.5$ produces a positive current, while $\alpha>0.5$ produces a negative current. At $\alpha=0.5$, the potential is symmetric and the net current is approximately zero.

$\alpha < 0.5$

Positive current and preferential motion to the right.

$\alpha = 0.5$

Symmetric potential and approximately zero net current.

$\alpha > 0.5$

Negative current and preferential motion to the left.

The flashing interval $T_p$ also affects the resulting current. If the potential is switched too quickly, particles have little time to respond to the potential landscape. For intermediate flashing intervals, a stronger directed current can emerge, while very large flashing intervals reduce the current again.

03

Theoretical and Numerical Results

The numerical simulations allow us to investigate how the current depends on the asymmetry, flashing time and temperature. These results can also be compared with an analytical expression for the average current.

$$ J_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{N_x}{4T_p} \left[ \operatorname{erfc} \left( \frac{\alpha N_x}{2} \sqrt{\frac{3}{T_p}} \right) - \operatorname{erfc} \left( \frac{(1-\alpha)N_x}{2} \sqrt{\frac{3}{T_p}} \right) \right]. $$

For the low-temperature case investigated in the project, the numerical and analytical results show good overall agreement for a range of asymmetry parameters. Small deviations arise from the discrete lattice, the finite number of particles and stochastic fluctuations between simulations.

We also investigate the effect of the inverse temperature parameter $\beta$. For small $\beta$, thermal fluctuations dominate the motion and the numerical current approaches zero. As $\beta$ increases, the potential has a stronger influence on the particle dynamics.

For the parameter range studied, the numerical results become increasingly similar to the analytical prediction as $\beta$ increases. The analytical expression does not explicitly contain $\beta$, allowing us to investigate how temperature affects the numerical transport relative to the theoretical approximation.

Key observation

Directed transport does not require deterministic particle motion. Stochastic thermal motion combined with spatial asymmetry and time-dependent switching can produce a measurable net current.

04

Conclusion

The simulations demonstrate how directed transport can emerge from stochastic particle motion. By combining thermal fluctuations with an asymmetric potential and periodic switching, the flashing-ratchet model produces a net current even though the individual particle movements remain random.

The results show that the direction and magnitude of the current depend on the spatial asymmetry, the flashing interval and the strength of thermal fluctuations. The numerical simulations also show reasonable agreement with the analytical prediction within the parameter regime investigated.

The model is deliberately simplified and does not reproduce the full molecular complexity of biological motor proteins. Real molecular motors involve chemical reactions, conformational changes, multiple interacting states and complex cellular environments. Nevertheless, the model demonstrates an important physical principle: stochastic thermal motion can be converted into directed transport when spatial asymmetry and time-dependent driving break the symmetry of the system.

Overall, the project illustrates how a relatively simple stochastic model can provide insight into the physical principles behind directed transport in biological systems.
