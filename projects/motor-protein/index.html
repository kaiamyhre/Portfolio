Brownian motion describes the seemingly random movement of microscopic particles caused by thermal fluctuations in their surroundings. While this motion has no preferred direction on its own, biological systems such as molecular motors are able to convert these fluctuations into directed transport.

In this project, we investigate how an asymmetric and time-dependent potential can produce a net particle current from otherwise random motion. We first establish the connection between diffusion, thermal energy and particle motion before developing a numerical model of a Brownian ratchet.

Finally, the numerical results are compared with an analytical solution to determine when the simplified theoretical model provides an accurate description of the system.

Main question

How can stochastic thermal motion produce directed transport when interacting with an asymmetric, time-dependent potential landscape?

01

From Diffusion to Particle Motion

The starting point is the one-dimensional diffusion equation,

$$ \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t} = D \frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial x^2}, $$

which describes how a particle distribution spreads with time. In the absence of an external potential, repeated random motion causes an initially localized collection of particles to spread into an approximately Gaussian distribution.

Introducing a potential $V(x)$ changes the probability of the possible movements. Using the Boltzmann distribution,

$$ P(x) \propto e^{-\beta V(x)}, \qquad \beta = \frac{1}{k_B T}, $$

we calculate the probability that a particle moves to the left, remains stationary, or moves to the right based on the potential energy of the neighboring positions.

$$ p^+ = \frac{e^{-\beta V(x+1)}} { e^{-\beta V(x-1)} + e^{-\beta V(x)} + e^{-\beta V(x+1)} } $$
$$ p^- = \frac{e^{-\beta V(x-1)}} { e^{-\beta V(x-1)} + e^{-\beta V(x)} + e^{-\beta V(x+1)} } $$
$$ p^0 = 1-p^+-p^-. $$

This introduces an important competition between thermal fluctuations and the potential landscape. At high temperatures, thermal energy dominates and the particles move almost independently of the potential. At low temperatures, the potential becomes increasingly important and particles tend to remain near local energy minima.

Numerical simulation in different potential landscapes

To investigate this behavior computationally, we developed a stochastic particle simulation in Python using NumPy, SciPy and Matplotlib. For each time step, the local potential determines the probabilities of moving left or right, while a randomly generated number determines the actual movement.

We initially simulated 10,000 particles in several different potential landscapes. A constant potential produced approximately symmetric diffusion, while a linear potential biased the movement in one direction. More complex potentials demonstrated how particles can become trapped around local energy minima.

01

$$ V(x)=k $$

Produces approximately unbiased diffusion.

02

$$ V(x)=-kx $$

Biases the particle motion in one direction.

03

Creates repeated local energy minima and barriers.

04

Quartic potential

$$ V(x)=kx^4 $$

Strongly confines particles around the centre.

These simulations demonstrate that random thermal motion alone does not create persistent directed transport. A mechanism that breaks the spatial symmetry of the system is required.

02

Generating Directed Motion with a Brownian Ratchet

The central part of the project investigates a periodically switching potential. The particles alternate between two states: a constant potential $V_2$, where they undergo approximately free diffusion, and an asymmetric sawtooth potential $V_1$.

While $V_2$ is active, particles diffuse randomly in both directions. When the asymmetric potential is restored, the particles move toward the nearest potential minimum. Because the distances to neighboring minima are unequal, the probability of ending up on either side is no longer symmetric.

Repeated switching therefore converts random Brownian motion into a net directional particle current.

1

Diffuse

Particles spread randomly while the potential is flat.

2

Switch on

The asymmetric potential is restored.

3

Relax

Particles move towards their nearest potential minimum.

4

Repeat

Repeated cycles produce a net displacement.

The trajectories illustrate the two regimes directly. During the free-diffusion stage the particles fluctuate randomly. When the sawtooth potential is activated, they move toward nearby minima, producing the characteristic step-like motion seen in the simulation.

03

Controlling the Particle Current

To quantify the directed transport, we define the normalized particle current at each time step as

$$ J(t_i) = \frac{n^+(t_i)-n^-(t_i)}{N_p}, $$

where $n^+$ and $n^-$ are the number of particles moving to the right and left, respectively, and $N_p$ is the total number of particles.

Potential asymmetry

The direction of the current is controlled by the asymmetry parameter $\alpha$. Changing $\alpha$ changes the shape of the sawtooth potential and therefore determines which direction is favored.

$\alpha < 0.5$

Positive current and preferential motion to the right.

$\alpha = 0.5$

Symmetric potential and approximately zero net current.

$\alpha > 0.5$

Negative current and preferential motion to the left.

Dependence on the switching period

Directed transport also depends strongly on how long the particles are allowed to diffuse before the asymmetric potential is restored.

For very short switching periods, particles have insufficient time to diffuse away from their initial minima and the resulting current remains close to zero. Increasing the period gives the particles more time to spread, allowing the asymmetric potential to generate a stronger net current.

For sufficiently long periods, the behavior changes again as the particle distribution has more time to adjust before the potential switches. The simulation therefore demonstrates that directed transport is not determined by spatial asymmetry alone, but also by the timescale at which the system is driven.

04

Numerical and Analytical Comparison

To evaluate the numerical model, we compare the simulated average current with an analytical expression,

$$ J_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{N_x}{4T_p} \left[ \operatorname{erfc} \left( \frac{\alpha N_x}{2} \sqrt{\frac{3}{T_p}} \right) - \operatorname{erfc} \left( \frac{(1-\alpha)N_x}{2} \sqrt{\frac{3}{T_p}} \right) \right]. $$

At low temperatures, the numerical and analytical solutions show very good agreement when varying the asymmetry of the potential. Both describe the reversal of the particle current as $\alpha$ passes through the symmetric configuration at $\alpha=0.5$.

The comparison also reveals the limitations of the analytical approximation. As thermal energy increases, particles become less influenced by the potential and the numerical current approaches zero. The analytical expression does not explicitly contain the temperature parameter $\beta$, and consequently does not reproduce this behavior.

The numerical results also contain stochastic fluctuations and discretization effects that are absent from the continuous analytical model. These differences become particularly visible for smaller particle populations and for parameter regimes where the assumptions behind the analytical approximation are less appropriate.

As $\beta$ increases and the thermal energy decreases, the particles respond more strongly to the potential and the numerical result approaches the analytical prediction. The comparison therefore allows us not only to validate the simulation, but also to identify where the assumptions behind the analytical model begin to break down.

Key observation

The analytical model provides a good description in the low-temperature regime, while the stochastic simulation allows us to investigate behavior outside the assumptions of the analytical approximation.

05

Conclusion

The simulations demonstrate how directed transport can emerge from fundamentally random particle motion. Brownian motion itself has no preferred direction, but combining thermal fluctuations with a periodically switching asymmetric potential breaks this symmetry and produces a measurable particle current.

The direction and magnitude of this current depend on the shape of the potential, the thermal energy and the switching timescale. Comparison with the analytical model further shows both where the simplified theoretical description performs well and where a numerical stochastic model is necessary to capture the behavior of the system.

Through the project, we combined statistical physics, mathematical modelling and numerical simulation to connect microscopic random motion with macroscopic directed transport and investigate a simplified physical model for motor protein motion.
