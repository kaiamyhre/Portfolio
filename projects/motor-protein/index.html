Modelling Motor Protein Motion

K. Myhre · S. Blom · L. Bekkely

Brownian motion describes the random movement of microscopic particles caused by thermal fluctuations. While this motion has no preferred direction on its own, biological systems such as molecular motors are able to convert these fluctuations into directed transport.

In this project, we investigate how an asymmetric and time-dependent potential can produce a net particle current from otherwise random motion. We develop a numerical model of a Brownian ratchet and compare the resulting particle current with an analytical solution.

Main question

How can stochastic thermal motion produce directed transport in an asymmetric, time-dependent potential?

From Diffusion to Particle Motion

At microscopic scales, thermal fluctuations produce irregular Brownian motion. The evolution of a particle distribution can be described by the one-dimensional diffusion equation,

$$ \frac{\partial \phi(x,t)}{\partial t} = D\frac{\partial^2\phi(x,t)}{\partial x^2}. $$

For an initial distribution $h(x)$, a solution is given by the Gaussian heat kernel,

$$ \phi(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} h(y) \exp\left( -\frac{(x-y)^2}{4Dt} \right) \,dy. $$

We verified that this expression satisfies the diffusion equation. As $t\rightarrow0$, the Gaussian kernel becomes localized around $x=y$, giving $\phi(x,0)=h(x)$. The function $h(x)$ therefore represents the initial particle distribution.

From diffusion to a random walk

In the numerical model, particles perform a random walk on a one-dimensional lattice. At each time step, a particle can move left, remain stationary, or move right. Introducing a potential $V(x)$ changes the probability of these transitions according to the Boltzmann distribution,

$$ P(x) = \frac{e^{-\beta V(x)}}{Z}, \qquad \beta=\frac{1}{k_BT}. $$

Applying this to the neighboring lattice positions gives

$$ \begin{aligned} p^+ &= \frac{e^{-\beta V(x+1)}}{Z_x}, \qquad p^0 = \frac{e^{-\beta V(x)}}{Z_x}, \qquad p^- = \frac{e^{-\beta V(x-1)}}{Z_x}, \\[4pt] Z_x &= e^{-\beta V(x-1)} +e^{-\beta V(x)} +e^{-\beta V(x+1)}. \end{aligned} $$

The parameter $\beta$ determines the competition between thermal fluctuations and the potential landscape. At high temperatures, $e^{-\beta\Delta V}\approx1$ and the three movements become approximately equally likely, $p^+\approx p^0\approx p^-\approx1/3$. At low temperatures, potential differences dominate and particles tend to remain near low-energy states.

Different potential landscapes

We implemented the stochastic model in Python using NumPy, SciPy and Matplotlib and initially simulated 10,000 particles in several potential landscapes. These cases show how the shape of $V(x)$ changes the resulting particle motion.

These simulations establish the two main ingredients of the Brownian-ratchet model: random thermal motion and a potential landscape capable of biasing that motion.

Generating Directed Motion

The central part of the project investigates a periodically switching potential. Particles alternate between a constant potential $V_2$, where they diffuse approximately freely, and an asymmetric periodic potential $V_1$ controlled by the parameter $\alpha$.

While $V_2$ is active, particles diffuse randomly in both directions. When $V_1$ is restored, they move toward nearby potential minima. Because the potential is asymmetric, the distances to neighboring minima are unequal, breaking the spatial symmetry of the system.

Repeated switching can therefore convert random Brownian motion into a net directional particle current.

Repeated cycles create net displacement.

The trajectories show the mechanism directly: random motion during free diffusion is followed by directed relaxation when the asymmetric potential is activated. Repeating these stages produces the characteristic step-like motion of the model.

Controlling the Particle Current

Directed transport is quantified using the normalized particle current,

$$ J = \frac{n^+-n^-}{N_p}, $$

where $n^+$ and $n^-$ are the numbers of rightward and leftward transitions. Positive and negative values therefore represent net transport in opposite directions.

Potential asymmetry

The direction of transport is controlled by the asymmetry parameter $\alpha$. Changing $\alpha$ changes the geometry of the periodic potential and can reverse the direction of the current.

$\alpha < 0.5$

Positive current and preferential motion to the right.

$\alpha = 0.5$

Symmetric potential and approximately zero current.

$\alpha > 0.5$

Negative current and preferential motion to the left.

Switching period

The current also depends on the switching period $T_p$. If the potential switches too quickly, particles have little time to diffuse and the current remains close to zero. At intermediate periods, diffusion allows more particles to cross into neighboring regions, producing a stronger net current.

For longer periods, the current decreases or stabilizes as the particles have more time to redistribute. Directed transport therefore depends on both spatial asymmetry and switching timescale.

Numerical and Analytical Comparison

To evaluate the numerical model, we compare the simulated current with an analytical expression,

$$ J_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{N_x}{4T_p} \left[ \operatorname{erfc} \left( \frac{\alpha N_x}{2} \sqrt{\frac{3}{T_p}} \right) - \operatorname{erfc} \left( \frac{(1-\alpha)N_x}{2} \sqrt{\frac{3}{T_p}} \right) \right]. $$

At low temperatures, the numerical and analytical results show very good agreement as $\alpha$ is varied. Both reproduce the reversal of the current around the symmetric case $\alpha=0.5$.

Small deviations arise because the simulation uses a finite number of particles on a discrete lattice and therefore contains stochastic fluctuations that are absent from the continuous analytical model.

The role of temperature

Temperature also reveals a limitation of the analytical expression, which does not explicitly contain $\beta$. At small $\beta$, thermal fluctuations dominate and the numerical current approaches zero. As $\beta$ increases, the potential becomes more influential and the numerical results approach the analytical prediction.

Key observation

The analytical model agrees well with the simulation at low temperatures, while the numerical model also captures the temperature dependence of the particle dynamics.

Conclusion

The simulations demonstrate how random Brownian motion can produce directed transport when combined with a periodically switching asymmetric potential. The direction and magnitude of the resulting current depend on the potential asymmetry, temperature and switching period.

Comparison with the analytical model shows good agreement in the low-temperature regime, while the numerical simulation also captures stochastic and temperature-dependent effects beyond the analytical approximation.
