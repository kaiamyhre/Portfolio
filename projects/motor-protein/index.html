Computational Physics · Biophysics

Modelling Motor Protein Motion

Stochastic Dynamics and Directed Transport

S. Blom · L. Bekkely · K. Myhre

01

Introduction

Motor proteins play an important role in transporting molecular cargo within cells. Their ability to produce directed motion from thermal fluctuations makes them an interesting example of stochastic processes in biological systems.

In this project, we investigate how directed motion can emerge from random walks in asymmetric potential landscapes. By modelling the movement of particles under different potentials and temperatures, we study how thermal fluctuations and potential asymmetry influence the resulting particle dynamics.

The project combines analytical theory with numerical simulations to investigate particle distributions, net current, and the conditions required for directed transport.

Main question

How can a system with stochastic thermal motion produce directed transport when interacting with an asymmetric potential landscape?

02

Diffusion and stochastic motion

We begin by considering the diffusion equation. For an initial distribution $h(x)$, the solution can be written using the Gaussian heat kernel:

$$ \phi(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} h(y) \exp\left( -\frac{(x-y)^2}{4Dt} \right) \,dy $$

This solution satisfies

$$ \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t} = D \frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial x^2}. $$

As $t \rightarrow 0$, the Gaussian kernel approaches a Dirac delta distribution. The solution therefore approaches the initial distribution:

$$ \phi(x,t) \rightarrow h(x). $$

This provides the theoretical foundation for describing particle motion as a stochastic diffusion process.

03

Particles in potential landscapes

The particle dynamics are influenced by the potential energy landscape $V(x)$. At thermal equilibrium, the probability of finding a particle at a particular position follows the Boltzmann distribution:

$$ P(x) = \frac{e^{-\beta V(x)}}{Z}, $$

where $Z$ is the normalization constant and

$$ \beta = \frac{1}{k_B T}. $$

Here, $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant and $T$ is the temperature. Consequently, $\beta$ describes the relative importance of the potential energy compared with thermal fluctuations.

High temperature

When $k_B T$ is large compared with the potential-energy differences, thermal fluctuations dominate the dynamics. The particle behaves approximately like an unbiased random walker.

Low temperature

When $k_B T$ is small, the potential landscape becomes more important. Particles are more likely to remain in regions of low potential energy and are less likely to cross high barriers.

04

Random walks

The stochastic dynamics are implemented as a discrete random walk. At each time step, a particle can move one position to the left, remain at its current position, or move one position to the right.

The probabilities of these transitions depend on the potential energy at neighboring positions. This allows the random walk to incorporate both thermal fluctuations and the underlying energy landscape.

$$ p^+ = \frac{e^{-\beta V(x+1)}} {e^{-\beta V(x-1)} +e^{-\beta V(x)} +e^{-\beta V(x+1)}} $$
$$ p^- = \frac{e^{-\beta V(x-1)}} {e^{-\beta V(x-1)} +e^{-\beta V(x)} +e^{-\beta V(x+1)}}. $$

The probability of remaining at the same position is obtained from normalization:

$$ p^0 = 1-p^+-p^-. $$

For a constant potential, the three probabilities are equal. The resulting motion therefore corresponds to an unbiased random walk.

05

Different potential landscapes

To investigate how the potential influences particle motion, several different landscapes were considered. These include a constant potential, a linear potential, a periodic asymmetric potential, and a quartic potential.

Constant potential

$$ V_1(x)=k $$

No preferred direction. The particle performs approximately unbiased diffusion.

Linear potential

$$ V_2(x)=-kx $$

The potential introduces a preferred direction for the particle.

Periodic potential

A spatially varying landscape containing potential wells and barriers.

Quartic potential

$$ V_4(x)=kx^4 $$

The rapidly increasing potential confines particles close to the centre.

06

The role of temperature

The parameter $\beta=1/(k_B T)$ controls the influence of the potential relative to thermal fluctuations.

For small $\beta$, corresponding to high thermal energy, particles can move more freely between regions of the potential landscape. For large $\beta$, corresponding to lower thermal energy, the particles become increasingly influenced by the potential minima.

Small $\beta$ Thermal fluctuations dominate
→
Large $\beta$ Potential dominates
07

From random walks to directed transport

A central part of the project is the flashing ratchet model. The system alternates between two different states: a flat potential where particles can diffuse freely, and an asymmetric periodic potential where the particles interact with the potential landscape.

During the flat-potential phase, thermal fluctuations allow particles to spread out. When the asymmetric potential is switched on, the particles tend to move towards nearby potential minima.

Repeating this process can result in a net displacement even though the underlying particle motion is stochastic.

08

Asymmetric potentials and current

The direction of transport depends strongly on the asymmetry of the potential. The asymmetry is controlled by the parameter $\alpha$.

The average particle current is measured using the difference between the number of particles moving to the right and to the left:

$$ J = \frac{n^+ - n^-}{N_p}, $$

where $n^+$ and $n^-$ denote the number of rightward and leftward particle transitions, respectively, and $N_p$ is the total number of particles.

For a symmetric potential, $\alpha=0.5$, the average current is approximately zero. Breaking the symmetry produces a preferred direction of transport.

α < 0.5

Positive current and preferential motion to the right.

α = 0.5

Symmetric potential and approximately zero net current.

α > 0.5

Negative current and preferential motion to the left.

09

Effect of the flashing interval

The duration of each flashing phase also influences the resulting current. If the potential is switched too quickly, particles do not have enough time to respond to the potential landscape.

For intermediate flashing intervals, a stronger directed current emerges. If the flashing interval becomes very large, the current can decrease again as the particles have more time to approach equilibrium within each phase.

$$ J_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{N_x}{4T_p} \left[ \operatorname{erfc} \left( \frac{\alpha N_x}{2} \sqrt{\frac{3}{T_p}} \right) - \operatorname{erfc} \left( \frac{(1-\alpha)N_x}{2} \sqrt{\frac{3}{T_p}} \right) \right]. $$

This analytical expression provides a theoretical prediction for the average current and can be compared with the numerical simulations.

10

Numerical and analytical comparison

The numerical simulations were compared with the analytical expression for the average current. For a range of asymmetry parameters, the numerical and analytical results show good agreement.

Small deviations can occur because the numerical model uses a discrete lattice and a finite number of particles. Stochastic fluctuations also introduce variation between individual simulations.

The agreement changes when other parameters, such as the flashing interval and inverse temperature, are varied. In particular, the analytical expression does not capture all features of the numerical model at high thermal energy.

11

Temperature dependence

The comparison between numerical and analytical results also depends on the inverse temperature parameter $\beta$. Increasing $\beta$ strengthens the influence of the potential relative to thermal fluctuations.

At very small $\beta$, the potential is weak compared with the thermal fluctuations and the numerical current approaches zero. For larger $\beta$, the numerical results become increasingly similar to the analytical prediction.

12

Limitations and interpretation

The analytical solution provides a useful description of the average transport behaviour, but it does not necessarily reproduce every feature of the numerical model.

Several effects can contribute to deviations between the two approaches. The numerical model uses a discrete spatial lattice and a finite number of particles, while the analytical treatment relies on approximations associated with the underlying diffusion process.

In addition, stochastic variation becomes more visible when the number of particles is limited. The temperature dependence of the numerical model can therefore lead to behaviour that is not fully represented by the analytical expression.

Key observation

Directed transport does not require deterministic particle motion. Instead, stochastic thermal motion combined with spatial asymmetry and time-dependent switching can produce a measurable net current.

13

Conclusion

This project investigated stochastic particle motion in different potential landscapes, with particular focus on the flashing ratchet mechanism for directed transport.

The simulations demonstrated how temperature, potential shape, asymmetry, and flashing time influence particle distributions and net current. In particular, asymmetric potentials can break the symmetry of stochastic motion and generate directed transport.

Comparing the numerical simulations with an analytical expression showed good agreement under suitable conditions, while also illustrating the limitations of analytical approximations when thermal fluctuations and numerical effects become important.
