Modelling Motor Protein Motion

Stochastic Dynamics and Directed Transport

K. Myhre · S. Blom · L. Bekkely

01

Introduction

Motor proteins play an important role in transporting molecular cargo within cells. Their ability to produce directed motion from thermal fluctuations makes them an interesting example of stochastic processes in biological systems.

In this project, we investigate how directed motion can emerge from random walks in asymmetric potential landscapes. By modelling the movement of particles under different potentials and temperatures, we study how thermal fluctuations and potential asymmetry influence particle dynamics.

Main question

How can stochastic thermal motion produce directed transport when interacting with an asymmetric potential landscape?

02

Diffusion and stochastic motion

We begin by considering the diffusion equation. For an initial distribution $h(x)$, the solution can be written using the Gaussian heat kernel:

$$ \phi(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} h(y) \exp\left( -\frac{(x-y)^2}{4Dt} \right) \,dy $$

This solution satisfies

$$ \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t} = D \frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial x^2}. $$

As $t \rightarrow 0$, the Gaussian kernel approaches a Dirac delta distribution:

$$ \phi(x,t) \rightarrow h(x). $$

This provides the theoretical foundation for describing particle motion as a stochastic diffusion process.

03

Particles in potential landscapes

The particle dynamics are influenced by the potential energy landscape $V(x)$. At thermal equilibrium, the probability of finding a particle at a particular position follows the Boltzmann distribution:

$$ P(x) = \frac{e^{-\beta V(x)}}{Z}, \qquad \beta = \frac{1}{k_B T}. $$

Here, $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant and $T$ is the temperature. The parameter $\beta$ describes the relative importance of the potential energy compared with thermal fluctuations.

High temperature

Thermal fluctuations dominate the dynamics and particles behave more like unbiased random walkers.

Low temperature

The potential becomes more important and particles are more strongly influenced by potential minima and barriers.

04

Random walks

The stochastic dynamics are implemented as a discrete random walk. At each time step, a particle can move left, remain at its current position, or move right.

The transition probabilities depend on the potential energy at neighbouring positions.

$$ p^+ = \frac{e^{-\beta V(x+1)}} {e^{-\beta V(x-1)} +e^{-\beta V(x)} +e^{-\beta V(x+1)}} $$
$$ p^- = \frac{e^{-\beta V(x-1)}} {e^{-\beta V(x-1)} +e^{-\beta V(x)} +e^{-\beta V(x+1)}} $$
$$ p^0 = 1-p^+-p^-. $$

For a constant potential, the three probabilities are equal. The resulting motion therefore corresponds to an unbiased random walk.

05

Several different landscapes were considered to investigate how the potential influences particle motion.

The rapidly increasing potential confines particles close to the centre.

07

From random walks to directed transport

A central part of the project is the flashing ratchet model. The system alternates between a flat potential, where particles can diffuse freely, and an asymmetric periodic potential.

During the flat-potential phase, thermal fluctuations allow particles to spread out. When the asymmetric potential is switched on, particles tend to move towards nearby potential minima.

Repeating this process can result in a net displacement even though the underlying particle motion is stochastic.

08

Asymmetric potentials and current

The direction of transport depends strongly on the asymmetry of the potential. The average particle current is measured using the difference between rightward and leftward transitions:

$$ J = \frac{n^+ - n^-}{N_p}. $$
α < 0.5

Positive current and preferential motion to the right.

α = 0.5

Symmetric potential and approximately zero net current.

α > 0.5

Negative current and preferential motion to the left.

09

Effect of the flashing interval

The duration of each flashing phase also influences the resulting current. If the potential is switched too quickly, particles do not have enough time to respond to the potential landscape.

For intermediate flashing intervals, a stronger directed current can emerge. If the flashing interval becomes very large, the current can decrease again.

$$ J_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{N_x}{4T_p} \left[ \operatorname{erfc} \left( \frac{\alpha N_x}{2} \sqrt{\frac{3}{T_p}} \right) - \operatorname{erfc} \left( \frac{(1-\alpha)N_x}{2} \sqrt{\frac{3}{T_p}} \right) \right]. $$

This analytical expression provides a theoretical prediction for the average current and can be compared with the numerical simulations.

10

Numerical and analytical comparison

The numerical simulations were compared with the analytical expression for the average current. For a range of asymmetry parameters, the numerical and analytical results show good agreement.

Small deviations can occur because the numerical model uses a discrete lattice and a finite number of particles. Stochastic fluctuations also introduce variation between simulations.

11

Temperature dependence

Increasing $\beta$ strengthens the influence of the potential relative to thermal fluctuations.

At very small $\beta$, the potential is weak compared with thermal fluctuations and the numerical current approaches zero. For larger $\beta$, the numerical results become increasingly similar to the analytical prediction.

12

Limitations and interpretation

The analytical solution provides a useful description of average transport behaviour, but it does not necessarily reproduce every feature of the numerical model.

Several effects can contribute to deviations between the two approaches. The numerical model uses a discrete spatial lattice and a finite number of particles, while the analytical treatment relies on approximations associated with the underlying diffusion process.

Key observation

Directed transport does not require deterministic particle motion. Stochastic thermal motion combined with spatial asymmetry and time-dependent switching can produce a measurable net current.

13

Conclusion

This project investigated stochastic particle motion in different potential landscapes, with particular focus on the flashing ratchet mechanism for directed transport.

The simulations demonstrated how temperature, potential shape, asymmetry, and flashing time influence particle distributions and net current.

Comparing the numerical simulations with an analytical expression showed agreement under suitable conditions, while also illustrating the limitations of analytical approximations.
