```
Motor proteins are molecular machines that transport cargo along cytoskeletal filaments inside cells. At these microscopic length scales, thermal fluctuations play an important role and the motion of individual particles is inherently stochastic. Despite this randomness, motor proteins are capable of producing persistent motion in a preferred direction.

In this project, we investigate how directed transport can emerge from stochastic particle motion. We begin by considering diffusion and the effect of different potential-energy landscapes on Brownian particles. We then construct a numerical model in which particles alternate between free diffusion and an asymmetric periodic potential. This provides a simplified model for how random thermal motion can be converted into directed transport.

Finally, we investigate how the resulting particle current depends on the shape of the potential, the switching period and the thermal energy of the system, and compare the numerical simulations with an analytical solution.

```
```

Stochastic particle motion

We begin with the one-dimensional diffusion equation

$$ \frac{\partial \phi(x,t)}{\partial t} = D\frac{\partial^2\phi(x,t)}{\partial x^2}, $$

where $\phi(x,t)$ describes the particle distribution and $D$ is the diffusion coefficient. The solution can be written as

$$ \phi(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} h(y) e^{-\frac{(x-y)^2}{4Dt}}\,dy, $$

where $h(x)$ represents the initial particle distribution. As time increases, an initially localized distribution spreads out as a consequence of random particle motion.

To introduce an external potential, we use the Boltzmann distribution. The probability of finding a particle at a position with potential energy $V(x)$ is proportional to

$$ P(x) \propto e^{-\beta V(x)}, \qquad \beta = \frac{1}{k_B T}. $$

We model the motion on a discrete spatial grid, where a particle can move one step to the left, remain stationary, or move one step to the right. The local potential determines the probability of each outcome. For example, the probability of moving to the right is

$$ p^+ = \frac{e^{-\beta V(x+1)}} {e^{-\beta V(x-1)} +e^{-\beta V(x)} +e^{-\beta V(x+1)}}. $$

This establishes a competition between thermal energy and the potential landscape. At high temperatures, $\beta$ is small and the differences in potential energy become relatively unimportant. The particle therefore approaches an unbiased random walk. At low temperatures, $\beta$ becomes large and the particles are increasingly restricted to regions of low potential energy.
```

```
Numerical model

To study this behavior computationally, we implemented a stochastic particle simulation in Python using NumPy, SciPy and Matplotlib. For every time step, the probabilities of moving left and right are calculated from the local potential. A uniformly distributed random number then determines the actual movement of each particle.

We first simulated 10,000 particles in several different potential landscapes. A constant potential produces unbiased random motion and an approximately Gaussian particle distribution. Introducing a slope into the potential biases the movement in one direction, while potentials containing local minima can confine particles to particular regions.

A strongly confining potential illustrates the effect particularly clearly. Even when the thermal energy is relatively high, particles can only diffuse a limited distance before the rapidly increasing potential makes further movement unlikely. As the temperature decreases, the distribution becomes increasingly concentrated around the potential minimum.

These simulations demonstrate that thermal fluctuations generate random motion, while the potential landscape determines which movements are energetically favorable. However, a static symmetric system does not by itself explain how persistent transport in one direction can emerge.

```
```

From Brownian motion to directed transport

To generate directed motion, we introduce a periodically switching potential. The particles alternate between two potential landscapes. The first, $V_1(x)$, is an asymmetric periodic sawtooth potential, while $V_2$ is constant.

The asymmetry of the sawtooth potential is controlled by the parameter $\alpha$. One period of the potential is described by

$$ V_1(x) = \begin{cases} \displaystyle \frac{kx}{\alpha N_x}, & 0 < x \leq \alpha N_x, \\[8pt] \displaystyle -\frac{kx}{(1-\alpha)N_x}, & -(1-\alpha)N_x \leq x \leq 0. \end{cases} $$

While the constant potential $V_2$ is active, there is no preferred direction and the particles diffuse randomly. After a time $T_p$, the asymmetric potential is restored. The particles are then driven toward the nearest potential minimum.

Because the potential is asymmetric, the distances to the neighboring minima are different. The particles therefore do not have equal probabilities of reaching the minima on either side. Repeatedly switching between free diffusion and the asymmetric potential converts the stochastic motion into a net directional current.

The trajectories make the two regimes visible. During the constant-potential stage, the particles fluctuate randomly. When $V_1$ is activated, they move toward nearby minima, producing the characteristic step-like motion seen in the simulation.

```
```

Controlling the particle current

To quantify the directed transport, we define the normalized particle current at each time step as

$$ J(t_i) = \frac{n^+(t_i)-n^-(t_i)}{N_p}, $$

where $n^+$ and $n^-$ are the number of particles moving to the right and left, respectively, and $N_p$ is the total number of particles.

The direction of this current is controlled by the asymmetry parameter $\alpha$. For $\alpha<0.5$, the potential favors transport in the positive direction, while $\alpha>0.5$ reverses the asymmetry and produces a negative current. At $\alpha=0.5$, the potential becomes symmetric and the average current approaches zero.

The switching period $T_p$ also plays an important role. If the period is very short, particles have little time to diffuse before the asymmetric potential is restored. Their displacement is therefore small and the resulting average current remains close to zero.

Increasing $T_p$ allows the particles to diffuse farther from their initial minima, making the asymmetry of the potential more effective at producing directed transport. For sufficiently long periods, the current begins to decrease again as the particles have more time to redistribute before the potential switches.

```

```
Numerical and analytical comparison

To validate the numerical model, we compare the simulated particle current with an analytical expression for the average current,

$$ J_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{N_x}{4T_p} \left[ \operatorname{erfc} \left( \frac{\alpha N_x}{2} \sqrt{\frac{3}{T_p}} \right) - \operatorname{erfc} \left( \frac{(1-\alpha)N_x}{2} \sqrt{\frac{3}{T_p}} \right) \right]. $$

At low temperatures, the numerical and analytical solutions show very good agreement when the asymmetry parameter is varied. Both predict a reversal of the particle current as $\alpha$ passes through the symmetric configuration at $\alpha=0.5$.

The comparison also exposes the limitations of the analytical model. The analytical expression does not explicitly contain the temperature parameter $\beta$. In the numerical model, however, temperature strongly affects the particle current. At high thermal energy, the particles become increasingly insensitive to the potential and their motion approaches an unbiased random walk, causing the net current to approach zero.

As $\beta$ increases and the thermal energy decreases, the particles respond more strongly to the potential and the numerical result approaches the analytical prediction. The numerical solution also contains stochastic fluctuations and discretization effects that are absent from the continuous analytical model.

The agreement therefore depends on the parameter regime. Rather than simply reproducing the analytical result, the simulation makes it possible to investigate when the assumptions behind the analytical approximation begin to break down.
```

```
Conclusion

This project demonstrates how directed transport can emerge from fundamentally stochastic particle motion. Thermal fluctuations alone produce Brownian motion without a preferred direction, but combining this motion with a periodically switching asymmetric potential breaks the spatial symmetry of the system and generates a measurable particle current.

The simulations show that both the direction and magnitude of this current depend on the asymmetry of the potential, the switching timescale and the thermal energy. By comparing the stochastic simulation with an analytical solution, we could also identify the regimes in which the simplified theoretical model provides a good approximation and where its assumptions become insufficient.

Through the project, we combined statistical physics, mathematical modelling and numerical simulation to investigate how microscopic random motion can be transformed into macroscopic directed transport, providing a simplified physical model for the mechanisms underlying motor protein motion.
```
