Modelling Motor Protein Motion

Stochastic Dynamics and Directed Transport
K. Myhre · S. Blom · L. Bekkely

Molecular motors are biological systems that convert chemical energy into directed mechanical motion. Their movement takes place in a highly fluctuating environment, where thermal noise plays an important role in determining the trajectory of individual particles.

This project develops a simplified computational model for studying how stochastic motion can produce directed transport. The model combines random thermal motion with an asymmetric potential landscape and periodically varying conditions.

Main question

How can stochastic thermal motion be transformed into directed transport in a simplified model of motor protein dynamics?

01

Diffusion and Random Walks

At microscopic scales, particles are continuously affected by collisions with surrounding molecules. These interactions produce random thermal motion, which can be described using Brownian motion and random walks.

In one dimension, the position of a particle can be represented by a sequence of random steps. For a simple symmetric random walk, each step has equal probability of moving to the left or right.

\[ x_{n+1} = x_n + \Delta x_n \]

Although the individual steps are unpredictable, the statistical behaviour of many particles follows well-defined relationships. In particular, the mean squared displacement grows approximately linearly with time:

\[ \langle x^2(t) \rangle = 2Dt \]

Here, \(D\) is the diffusion coefficient. This relation provides a simple way of identifying diffusive behaviour in numerical simulations.

The random-walk model forms the stochastic foundation of the motor protein model. However, random diffusion alone does not create a preferred direction of motion.

02

Potential Landscapes

To introduce structure into the stochastic motion, the particle is placed in a potential landscape. The potential determines which positions are energetically favourable and therefore affects the probability distribution of the particle.

At thermal equilibrium, the probability of finding a particle at position \(x\) follows the Boltzmann distribution:

\[ P(x) \propto e^{-\beta V(x)} \]

where

\[ \beta = \frac{1}{k_B T}. \]

Here \(V(x)\) is the potential energy, \(k_B\) is Boltzmann's constant, and \(T\) is the temperature.

Symmetric Potential

A symmetric potential produces no preferred direction. The resulting equilibrium distribution is therefore symmetric.

\[ V(-x)=V(x) \]

Asymmetric Potential

An asymmetric potential contains regions with different slopes or barrier widths. This asymmetry becomes important when combined with time-dependent forcing.

\[ V(-x)\neq V(x) \]

Temperature Dependence

Temperature controls the strength of thermal fluctuations. At low temperature, particles are more strongly concentrated around energetically favourable regions. At higher temperature, thermal fluctuations allow particles to explore a larger portion of the potential landscape.

03

Directed Transport

A central feature of the model is the combination of thermal fluctuations, spatial asymmetry, and periodic changes in the potential. This is commonly studied using a flashing ratchet model.

The potential is periodically switched between an active state and an inactive state. When the potential is active, the particle experiences the asymmetric landscape. When it is switched off, thermal diffusion allows the particle to redistribute.

\[ V(x,t)= \begin{cases} V(x), & \text{potential on} \\ 0, & \text{potential off} \end{cases} \]

Because the potential is asymmetric, the redistribution during the off-state does not necessarily cancel the motion that occurred while the potential was active.

Effect of Asymmetry

The degree of asymmetry can be controlled by a parameter such as \(\alpha\). Changing this parameter modifies the shape of the potential and therefore changes the resulting transport.

Flashing Interval

The switching frequency also affects the transport. If the potential is switched too quickly, the particle has limited time to respond. If it is switched too slowly, the system spends longer periods in states where directed transport is reduced.

04

Numerical Results

The model was investigated numerically by simulating particle trajectories over many time steps. Ensemble averages were then used to estimate quantities such as the mean position, mean squared displacement, and transport current.

The transport current can be estimated from the long-time change in the average particle position:

\[ J = \lim_{t\rightarrow\infty} \frac{\langle x(t)-x(0)\rangle}{t}. \]

A non-zero value of \(J\) indicates that the average motion has a preferred direction. This allows the simulations to distinguish ordinary diffusion from directed transport.

Temperature Effects

Temperature changes the balance between thermal exploration and confinement by the potential. The simulations therefore show how the transport current depends on the strength of thermal fluctuations.

Key observation The simulations demonstrate that random thermal motion does not necessarily prevent directed transport. When stochastic fluctuations interact with an asymmetric and time-dependent potential, a net transport current can emerge.

The model is deliberately simplified and does not reproduce the full molecular complexity of biological motor proteins. Real molecular motors involve chemical reactions, conformational changes, multiple interacting states, and complex cellular environments.

Nevertheless, the model illustrates an important physical principle: directed motion can emerge from stochastic dynamics when spatial asymmetry and time-dependent driving break the symmetry of otherwise random motion.
