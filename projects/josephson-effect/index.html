The Josephson Effect

Numerical Modelling of an SNS Junction

Superconductors can carry electrical current without resistance. Even when two superconductors are separated by a normal metal, superconducting correlations can penetrate the intervening material. Under suitable conditions, a phase difference between the superconductors produces a current through the junction without an applied voltage: the Josephson effect.

In this project, we numerically investigate a superconductor–normal metal–superconductor (SNS) junction. We first develop numerical techniques for differential equations, then solve the Usadel equations to study the local density of states and the phase-dependent supercurrent.

Main question

How do superconducting correlations and a phase difference generate a current through a normal-metal junction?

Numerical Methods and Validation

We begin by testing an adaptive Runge–Kutta method on a second-order differential equation with a known analytical solution:

$$y''(x)=-4\sin(2x),\qquad y(0)=0,\qquad y'(0)=2.$$

Introducing $y_1=y$ and $y_2=y'$ gives a first-order system,

$$\frac{d}{dx}\begin{pmatrix}y_1\\y_2\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}y_2\\-4\sin(2x)\end{pmatrix}, \qquad y(x)=\sin(2x).$$

The adaptive method adjusts its step size according to the requested error tolerance. Comparing the numerical and analytical solutions provides a direct accuracy check.

Boundary value problems

We also use the secant method to estimate unknown initial derivatives and compare a shooting approach with SciPy's solve_bvp. This prepares the numerical framework for the coupled boundary value problem describing the SNS junction.

Modelling the SNS Junction

The physical system consists of a normal metal placed between two superconducting electrodes. In the diffusive regime, the Usadel equations describe how superconducting correlations evolve through the junction. We use a Riccati parametrisation involving the complex $2\times2$ matrices $\gamma$, $\tilde{\gamma}$, $\omega$ and $\tilde{\omega}$.

$$\begin{aligned} \partial_x\gamma &= \omega,\\ \partial_x\tilde{\gamma} &= \tilde{\omega},\\ \partial_x\omega &= -2i(\epsilon+i\delta)\gamma -2\omega\tilde N\tilde{\gamma}\omega,\\ \partial_x\tilde{\omega} &= -2i(\epsilon+i\delta)\tilde{\gamma} -2\tilde{\omega}N\gamma\tilde{\omega}. \end{aligned}$$

Here $\epsilon$ denotes energy, $\delta$ is a small broadening parameter, and the auxiliary matrices are

$$N=(I-\gamma\tilde{\gamma})^{-1}, \qquad \tilde N=(I-\tilde{\gamma}\gamma)^{-1}.$$

The Superconducting Proximity Effect

A normal metal placed next to a superconductor acquires superconducting correlations, even though the material itself is not superconducting. We examine this proximity effect through the local density of states, $D(\epsilon,x)/D_0$, which measures the available electronic states relative to the normal-metal reference.

$$\frac{D(\epsilon,x)}{D_0} =\frac{1}{4}\operatorname{Re} \left[\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(\hat\rho_3\hat g(x,\epsilon)\bigr)\right].$$

In the normal-metal reference system, this quantity equals one. Superconducting boundary conditions introduce an energy-dependent structure, showing how the electrodes influence the normal region.

Key observation

The normal-metal density of states is modified by the presence of the superconductors, providing a numerical signature of the proximity effect.

Supercurrent Through the Junction

The superconducting electrodes have phases $\phi_L$ and $\phi_R$. Their difference, $\Delta\phi=\phi_L-\phi_R$, controls the equilibrium supercurrent through the normal metal.

$\Delta\phi=0$

The calculated current is approximately zero.

$\Delta\phi\ne0$

A phase-driven supercurrent appears in the junction.

Conservation

For a fixed energy, the current should remain approximately independent of position.

Energy dependence

With a nonzero phase difference, we calculate the spectral current over a range of energies. Its magnitude and sign depend on energy, reflecting the contribution of different electronic states.

Checking current conservation

We evaluate the current at different positions along the normal metal to check the consistency of the numerical solution. The current is approximately spatially conserved, as expected for the stationary model.

The Current–Phase Relationship

Finally, we repeat the calculation for different phase differences and integrate the energy-dependent current to obtain the total phase-dependent response. A characteristic Josephson relationship is

$$I(\Delta\phi)=I_c\sin(\Delta\phi),$$

where $I_c$ is the critical current. The numerical curve is approximately sinusoidal: it vanishes near $0$ and $2\pi$, with opposite extrema around $\pi/2$ and $3\pi/2$.

Main result

A phase difference between the superconductors generates a supercurrent whose phase dependence is approximately sinusoidal, consistent with the Josephson effect.

Conclusion

By combining numerical differential-equation methods with the Usadel model, we investigated superconducting correlations in an SNS junction. The calculations reproduce the normal-metal reference, show how superconducting electrodes modify the local density of states, and demonstrate a phase-driven current that is approximately conserved throughout the junction. Most importantly, the computed current–phase relation resembles the sinusoidal behaviour expected from the Josephson effect.
